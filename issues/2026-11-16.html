The integers are Diophantine in the rationals, say a week of human proofs

  • Preprints: Burungale, Sun, Kozachinskiy–Pasten–Vidaux, Glazer et al.
  • Company claim: OpenAI’s undecidability manuscript
  • Expert review: independent proofs, no verdict yet

OpenAI’s 6 October release of 722 machine-written manuscripts included a 62-page paper claiming a negative answer to Hilbert’s tenth problem over ℚ: no algorithm decides whether an integer polynomial has a rational zero. It has no Lean proof and no outside review. On 5 November, two AI-generated preprints went further and claimed that ℤ itself is Diophantine in ℚ. One, by Sohail Farhangi, is conditional on three OpenAI papers. The other, by Elliot Glazer, is self-contained. Glazer credits OpenAI’s Astra model and Claude, and his defining polynomial had 292,890,231,905 variables.

This week the polynomial shrank and human proofs appeared. In Glazer’s public repository, collaborators including Gabriel Conant, Jose Brox, Cameron Freer, James Freitag and Caroline Terry cut it to 299 variables by 9 November, then 214, and now 196 in Brox’s version, all at degree four. On 10 November, Zhi-Wei Sun posted a 26-unknown version, improving on a 221-unknown count by Conant and coauthors. He says 16 unknowns suffice if the abc conjecture holds. On 12 November, the number theorist Ashay Burungale posted an unconditional proof. He credits OpenAI’s work as inspiration but says his proof does not use the new inputs OpenAI announced, a “2-converse” theorem and a 2-adic Fontaine–Mazur result. The same day, Alexander Kozachinskiy, Hector Pasten and Xavier Vidaux posted a conditional definition, assuming abc, Dickson’s conjecture on prime patterns, and finiteness of the 2-parts of Shafarevich–Tate groups. They describe their paper as “an entry point” to the new work.

The intuition: making a polynomial count

  1. Diophantine means “a shadow of a solution set”. A set S of rationals is Diophantine if q is in S exactly when some fixed equation P(q, w1, …, wm) = 0 has a rational solution w. For example, the nonnegative rationals are Diophantine, because q ≥ 0 exactly when q is a sum of four rational squares.
  2. Why ℤ is the prize. Matiyasevich proved in 1970, completing work by Davis, Putnam and Robinson, that every listable set of integers is Diophantine over ℤ, so integer solvability is undecidable. If ℤ is Diophantine in ℚ, you can take any integer equation, add the condition “each unknown is an integer” using the extra variables of P, and get a rational equation that is solvable exactly when the original one is. So rational solvability is undecidable too.
  3. The last quantifier. Julia Robinson defined ℤ inside ℚ in 1949, but her definition used “for all” as well as “there exists”. Poonen in 2009 and Koenigsmann in 2016 cut the number of universal quantifiers down to one, which is the same as saying the non-integers are Diophantine. The open problem was removing that last “for all”. A conjecture of Barry Mazur pointed the other way. It predicts that the rational points of any variety are tame on the real line, falling into finitely many pieces, whereas ℤ is infinitely many isolated points. If the new proofs hold, Mazur’s conjecture is false.
  4. Elliptic curves can count. Take an elliptic curve whose rational points are generated by a single point P, so the multiples nP list them all. The denominators of nP form a divisibility sequence: a prime p divides the denominator exactly when n is a multiple of a fixed number that depends on p. Divisibility of denominators therefore records divisibility of the index n, and this is how integer arithmetic gets into ℚ. The route goes back to Thanases Pheidas.
  5. Closing the gap. Local conditions at well-chosen primes force a free rational parameter t to lie close to some index n. A height argument then leaves only finitely many non-integers, which are handled separately. Burungale needs an infinite supply of curves with rank exactly one. He gets them from quadratic twists of the congruent-number curve, using a rank-one criterion of Tian, Yuan and Zhang, and he finds the right primes with Green, Tao and Ziegler’s theorem on prime patterns.

What it does and does not mean

Everything here is a preprint posted within the past ten days, and I found no specialist verdict on any of them. Glazer’s Lean formalization is incomplete: the main declaration still contains a sorry. The repository itself says its AI audits and finite checks are not peer review. OpenAI’s original manuscript is still unformalized and unreviewed. Sun’s abstract presents his result as an improvement on the Conant group’s count, not as an independent proof. For cryptography, nothing changes. Undecidability is a worst-case statement about all equations at once. Cryptography relies on particular equations, such as elliptic curves over finite fields, and none of this makes any particular equation easier to solve. My read: the strongest evidence so far is Burungale’s independent route, which doesn’t depend on OpenAI’s new inputs. The pattern is new too: an AI claim named a target and a route, and human proofs followed within five weeks. If they hold, Pasten and coauthors note, even the set of powers of two is Diophantine in ℚ, contradicting a conjecture of Corvaja and Zannier.

Formalization week: Fermat in a day, Faltings, Weil and ternary Goldbach

  • Company claim: Anthropic, Axiom
  • Machine-checked: Lean, no outside rebuild yet

On 9 November, Anthropic described a toy experiment in which 1,000 agents running an early version of Claude Fable 5.2 reproved Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in 28.7 hours, with no researcher steering. The agents were given 59 milestone statements, a proof sketch and 47 lemmas from September’s run. That earlier proof took dozens of agents 11 days and runs to 13 million lines. The new one is about a seventh of that length. Axiom followed its 4 November Stacks Project release with the four Weil conjectures on 6 November and Faltings’ theorem (the Mordell conjecture) on 12 November. The Faltings proof is about 110,000 lines of Lean on top of some 6.4 million lines of Stacks library, and it follows the Bombieri–Vojta route. Separately, a public repository by the GitHub user calr3 now gives a sorry-free Lean proof of Helfgott’s 2013 theorem that every odd number above 5 is a sum of three primes. Quanta reported it on 10 November.

The intuition: what a Lean certificate actually certifies

  1. The kernel checks every step from Lean’s axioms. What it can’t check is everything you feed it: the statement, the definitions and any hypotheses.
  2. Goldbach comes with two hypotheses. The main theorem assumes two published computations, Platt’s numerical check of the generalized Riemann hypothesis and the verification of binary Goldbach up to 4·1018. Odd numbers below 1027 are handled by a ladder of about 250 million primes that the kernel itself checks, without the faster but less trusted native_decide.
  3. Bombieri–Vojta in one breath. Map the rational points of a curve of genus at least two into a lattice. Mumford’s gap principle says that points pointing in nearly the same direction have heights that grow geometrically. Vojta’s inequality, proved with an auxiliary polynomial, says two such points can’t both have large and very different heights. Together, the two leave only finitely many points.

My take: the Fermat rerun is the clearest signal. A directed 11-day campaign became an unsupervised one-day job, although the agents were handed the milestones. No outsider has rebuilt Axiom’s results or audited their statements, and the Goldbach README says its statement-comparison check hasn’t been rerun on the current version.

Sources: Anthropic, scaling multi-agent teams (9 Nov), Anthropic, September FLT formalization, Axiom, Faltings’ theorem, Axiom, Weil conjectures, ternary Goldbach repository, Quanta updates page.

SHA-256 collisions below the birthday bound reach 39 of 64 steps

  • Preprint: IACR ePrint 2026/2867

On 11 November, Jiaying Gong, Yongzhuang Wei, Fengrong Zhang and Bohua Wei (Guilin University of Electronic Technology and Xidian University) submitted a paper to ePrint, and it went public on 13 November. It estimates that collisions for SHA-256 reduced to 39 of its 64 steps cost about 2108.8 compressions per expected collision, below the generic 2128 birthday bound. The previous best classical collision attack, by Li and coauthors, reached 38 steps at about 2104.3, and this paper cuts that to 290.9. These are cost estimates. No collision was computed, and the paper doesn’t mention AI.

The intuition: stop gambling on the expanded message

A differential attack fixes a path for how a small difference moves through the steps, and each step adds conditions on the internal state. The first 16 message words are free to choose, but from step 16 on the words are computed from earlier ones, so conditions there usually have to be met by luck. The new trick, “joint message-word pinning”, picks state words that carry no direct conditions so that expanded words W16 and W17 come out exactly as the path needs.

For deployed systems: nothing changes. Full SHA-256 has 64 steps, so 25 steps of margin remain, and the attack costs are still astronomical. My take: a 13-bit gain at 38 steps suggests the step-reduced estimates still have slack. Watch the margin, not the headline.

Sources: Gong, Wei, Zhang and Wei, ePrint 2026/2867.

Balanced Boolean functions in 10 variables top out at nonlinearity 492

  • Preprint: IACR ePrint 2026/2797

Youle Xu and Qichun Wang posted the paper on 6 November and revised it on 11 November. It claims to settle Dobbertin’s 1994 conjecture in dimension 10. Nonlinearity is the distance from a Boolean function to the nearest affine function, and high nonlinearity helps ciphers resist linear cryptanalysis. In 10 variables, bent functions reach 496, but they are never balanced. Dobbertin’s construction gives balanced functions with 29 − 25 + 12 = 492, where 12 is the best value for balanced functions in 5 variables. The nonlinearity of a balanced function is even, so the only open value was 494, and the paper rules it out.

The intuition: Parseval leaves room, arithmetic closes it

The nonlinearity is 29 minus half the largest Walsh coefficient in absolute value. Parseval fixes the sum of the 1,024 squared coefficients at 220, so the largest must be at least 32. Nonlinearity 494 would need every coefficient to be at most 36 in absolute value, and counting alone allows that. The authors add a quadratic-residue constraint on the spectrum and track how energy splits across four 8-variable sub-functions, keeping signs. That leaves a single case, which a product inequality and a parity argument eliminate.

My take: this is a design bound with no effect on any deployed cipher. It’s a human result, and with its finite, case-heavy flavor, it’s a natural candidate for a Lean check.

Sources: Xu and Wang, ePrint 2026/2797.

Follow-up: the Yang–Mills Lean proof is public, with a precise scope

  • Company claim: OpenAI
  • Machine-checked: Lean, code now public
  • Expert review: OpenAI-appointed committee affirms existence only

What changed. On 10 November, the shepherding committee chaired by David Gross published two GitHub repositories, one with the full Lean code OpenAI submitted on 28 October and one with its assessment. The committee’s finding is that the code builds a relativistic quantum field theory satisfying the Wightman axioms, as a scaling limit of four-dimensional Wilson lattice gauge theory, for any simple compact gauge group. It does not show that the infinite-volume limit is unique or that there is a mass gap, and QCD isn’t addressed. The 1,147-page human-readable proof is still unreleased. Scott Armstrong of NYU welcomed the release but told Scientific American: “This review can be done more efficiently and quickly in the open.” The repository lists two more submissions as work in progress, a second one from OpenAI (4 November) and one from Levent Alpöge and Ethan Sussman (15 November).

The intuition: from a grid to a continuum

  1. Lattice. Wilson put the gauge field on the edges of a four-dimensional grid as group elements. On a finite grid, that gives an honest probability measure.
  2. Continuum limit. Shrink the spacing while tuning the coupling toward zero, as asymptotic freedom dictates, and show that the correlation functions converge.
  3. Reconstruction. Euclidean correlations that satisfy the Osterwalder–Schrader axioms, notably reflection positivity, rebuild a Hilbert space and a relativistic Wightman theory.
  4. What’s missing. The mass gap says every excitation above the vacuum costs a fixed minimum energy, which means correlations decay uniformly. Nothing here proves that.

My read: the existence half of the Millennium problem now has a public artifact that anyone can inspect. The open question has moved to whether the Lean definitions of the Wightman axioms are faithful. The committee says they match the standard problem statements, but OpenAI appointed it, so outside audits of those definitions are now the thing to watch.

Sources: committee assessment repository, OpenAI Lean submission, third committee statement, Scientific American (11 Nov).

DeepMind’s agents write quantum programs with Lean-checked correctness and cost

  • Preprint: arXiv 2611.09822
  • Company claim: Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI

On 6 November (revised 9 November), 25 authors, including Ryan Babbush, Sergio Boixo and Pushmeet Kohli, described a Lean library in which teams of Gemini 4 agents write quantum programs against formal specifications. Lean then proves that each program is correct and that its logical cost, measured as qubits times T gates, stays under a stated bound. The largest gain is a 25-bit fixed-point arcsine, used to load numbers into amplitudes, which needs 3,698 T gates on 100 qubits. That is 290 times cheaper than a published construction, though that construction targeted a looser error. Simulations relevant to fusion, photochemistry and drug metabolism get cheaper by about 11, 8 and 2.5 times. The library is “to be open sourced shortly”, so it isn’t public yet.

The intuition: you can’t test a quantum program, so prove it

A classical computer can only simulate small instances, so testing says little about a 4,000-qubit circuit. Here the specification states which unitary to approximate and to what error, and it requires the ancilla qubits to come back to zero. Quantum Hoare logic works backward from that postcondition to the weakest precondition the program needs. Because the checker verifies the cost bound too, an agent can’t win by cheating on either side.

My read: the costs are logical, before error-correction overhead, and the baselines are the authors’ choice. The paper doesn’t touch modular exponentiation, so RSA and elliptic-curve resource estimates for Shor’s algorithm are unchanged. The method is a natural fit for that arithmetic, though, and those estimates drive migration timelines.

Sources: Shirobokov et al., arXiv 2611.09822.

Welcome to Frontier Notes, issue four, for Monday, November sixteenth. I’m glad you’re here. This week, human mathematicians answer an AI claim about Hilbert’s tenth problem. After that, a busy week for computer-checked proofs, two cryptography results, the Yang–Mills code going public, and verified quantum programs.

The deep dive. In October, OpenAI claimed that no algorithm can decide whether a polynomial equation has a rational solution. This week, several mathematicians went further. They posted proofs that the whole numbers can be picked out of the fractions by a single polynomial equation of degree four.

The trick uses elliptic curves. Multiples of one point on a curve behave like a counter, and the primes dividing their denominators keep track of the count.

Ashay Burungale posted an independent proof, and Zhi-Wei Sun got the equation down to twenty-six unknowns. These are all preprints, with no expert verdict yet and no complete computer check. It changes nothing for deployed cryptography.

Next, formal proofs. Anthropic says a thousand Claude agents reproved Fermat’s Last Theorem in Lean in under thirty hours, a job that took eleven days in September. Axiom formalized Faltings’ theorem and the Weil conjectures. And the ternary Goldbach theorem now has a Lean proof that assumes two published computations. These are company claims with machine-checked proofs, and no outsider has rebuilt them yet.

In cryptanalysis, a preprint estimates that collisions for SHA-256, cut down to thirty-nine of its sixty-four steps, now cost less than a generic attack. The trick is to solve for two message words directly instead of hoping they come out right. Full SHA-256 is not affected.

Another preprint settles a question from cipher design. A balanced Boolean function of ten variables can have nonlinearity at most four hundred ninety-two, which confirms a conjecture from nineteen ninety-four.

Physics. The committee led by David Gross published OpenAI’s Lean code for Yang–Mills theory. This is a company claim with a machine-checked proof, affirmed by a committee that OpenAI appointed. The long human-readable proof is still not public.
