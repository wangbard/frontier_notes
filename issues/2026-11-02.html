Factoring goes subexponential, deterministically

  • Preprint: Hexagon
  • Machine-checked: 13-line Lean statement
  • Opinion: Elliot Glazer on X

On 28 October, Isaac Rudich and Louis-Martin Rousseau of Polytechnique Montréal posted “Deterministic Factoring in Time No(1)” to Hexagon, the preprint server for AI-assisted mathematics. The claim is a single deterministic machine that, for every fixed k, finds the smallest prime factor of an n-bit number N in at most Ck·2n/k steps, which is roughly N1/k. The ideas and the roughly 35,000-line Lean proof were generated by Claude Opus 5.5. The two authors designed and audited the 13-line formal statement, which depends only on Mathlib and Lean’s standard axioms, and they say they did not read the proof. The project began when they asked Claude to apply the new ETH results to factoring. No human-readable explanation exists yet.

Deterministic, provable factoring has advanced very slowly for fifty years. Trial division takes about N1/2 steps. Pollard and Strassen got this down to N1/4 in the 1970s. Hittmeir reached 2/9 in 2018 and Harvey reached 1/5 in 2021, and Harvey and Hittmeir later shaved off a log-log factor. Each of these bounds has a fixed positive exponent. The new result says the exponent can be pushed below any positive number. On X, mathematician Elliot Glazer summed it up as factoring now being “proven subexponential (deterministically, worst-case bound)”. He reported that an AI audit found the Lean proves exactly what it states, while the English write-up contains some errors.

The intuition: find a factor by counting, then count the way the ETH proof does

  1. Search by counting. Imagine an oracle that tells you how many divisors of N above 1 begin with a given string of leading bits. You can then build the smallest divisor one bit at a time. At each step, ask whether any divisor continues the current prefix with a 0. If one does, append 0, and otherwise append 1. That takes about n questions, and the smallest divisor above 1 is automatically prime.
  2. Counting divisors means counting solutions. A divisor with a given prefix is a pair of numbers x, y with x·y = N, where x matches the prefix. That’s a constraint problem on about n unknown bits. Counting solutions to constraint problems is exactly what last week’s ETH engine does in time 2εn.
  3. The catch: multiplication isn’t local. Write x·y = N as a 3-SAT formula and the carries force you to add on the order of n2 helper variables. The engine’s cost grows with the total number of variables, so 2ε·n² is worse than trial division. Per the README, the proof gets around this with its own multiplication network, so the costs scale with the n input bits. My read, since no write-up exists yet: the engine needs variables split into groups that interact only through a few small shared quantities. Long multiplication has natural candidates, because the carries passed between blocks of digits are small numbers.
  4. Deform, then interpolate. As last week, the engine adds a dial. At a few chosen settings the groups separate and can be counted by multiplying, and polynomial interpolation recovers the exact count at the original setting.
  5. Galactic constants. The constants Ck exist but are uncomputed, and the README calls them astronomically large.

What it means for RSA and the rest of deployed cryptography

For RSA, it changes nothing. RSA key sizes were never set by deterministic worst-case bounds. They are set by the number field sieve, which is randomized, relies on heuristics, and runs in roughly exp(1.92·(ln N)1/3(ln ln N)2/3) time. Even the best provable randomized method, from Lenstra and Pomerance in 1992, runs in about exp(√(ln N·ln ln N)). Asymptotically, that already beats N1/k for every fixed k. It would even beat the README’s stated (and unproved) target of exp(O(ln N/√(ln ln N))). Elliptic curves, Diffie–Hellman and ML-KEM are unaffected. What the result settles is a long-open theoretical question.

My read: the bigger signal is that the ETH engine is now a reusable tool, transplanted within days. The useful question is which problems have the right shape for it. Lattice problems mostly don’t. Their hardness is exponential in the dimension d, but an instance takes about d2·log q bits to write down, so an algorithm that is subexponential in the input length tells you nothing about them. More natural next targets are problems where the best deterministic bound is still a fixed power of the input size, such as discrete logarithms solved without randomness.

Sources: Rudich and Rousseau on Hexagon, Lean repository and README, Glazer on X, Harvey’s exponent-1/5 algorithm (arXiv).

The classification of finite simple groups, checked end to end by AI agents

  • Company claim: Axiom
  • Machine-checked, not yet rebuilt by outsiders
  • Preprint: Defant–Ono repair
  • Expert review: Lyons and Solomon advised on the repair

On 26 October the startup Axiom announced a complete Lean formalization of the classification of finite simple groups (CFSG). Its proof is spread over thousands of journal pages by more than a hundred authors. Axiom says a swarm of AI agents wrote about 43.1 million lines of Lean in 24 days. It follows the first-generation proof, with Aschbacher and Smith’s 2004 volumes for the quasithin case. Axiom logged 34 places where the published record deviates from a correct proof, mostly typos and dropped hypotheses. Two of these are real gaps. One is a lemma in the quasithin volumes that fails for the group SL2(5). The other is a closure claim in John Walter’s 1986 memoir on the Unbalanced Group Theorem. The team repaired the Walter gap by a new route, with advice from Richard Lyons and Ronald Solomon. Axiom mathematicians Colin Defant and Ken Ono posted the repair on Hexagon on 27 October.

The intuition: you can classify a group by looking at its involutions

The proof argues by contradiction. Take the smallest simple group that isn’t on the list. In the 1950s Brauer and Fowler showed that the centralizer of an involution, meaning the elements that commute with a given element of order 2, determines a simple group up to finitely many possibilities. Much of CFSG is therefore “local analysis” of such pieces. The Unbalanced Group Theorem handles the case where such a centralizer contains an awkward “unbalanced” component. Walter’s argument assumed that a certain family of simple groups stays inside the family under one step of this analysis. The agents found a counterexample in PSL2(49). The centralizer of its field involution is PGL2(7), and its simple part, PSL2(7), falls outside the family because 7 is a Mersenne prime. Nobody thinks the classification is false. The gap was in one route through the proof, and a new route now exists.

My take: Lean’s kernel checks every step, so you don’t have to trust the agents. You do have to trust the definitions, and Axiom says humans still need to confirm that the formal statement matches the classification. CEO Carina Hong told Runtime Wire that no outside group has rebuilt the proof yet, and that Axiom is publishing build artifacts so others can.

Sources: Axiom’s announcement, Defant and Ono, “The Unbalanced Group Theorem” (Hexagon), Runtime Wire on independent rebuilds.

Follow-up: specialists weigh in on the quasi-Riemann claim

  • Company claim: OpenAI
  • Machine-checked: 7/8 version only
  • Expert review: examining, with cautious support

What changed. Last week we listed this claim from OpenAI’s 6 October release as unverified. We missed a 19 October Quanta report by Konstantin Kakaes gathering specialist reactions. There are two versions. One is 199 pages, dated 30 September and verified in Lean. It puts every nontrivial zero of the zeta function between real parts 1/8 and 7/8. The other is 49 pages, dated 5 October and not formalized, and it proves the weaker strip from 1/12 to 11/12. Experts have focused on the short one. Maksym Radziwiłł says he can sketch its argument in half a page and calls the implications “staggering”. Alexander Dunn calls it a surprising synthesis of known ideas. Peter Sarnak won’t call it a theorem until humans have checked every detail. Quanta reports that experts largely agree the 11/12 result is correct, and that write-ups of 7 to 13 pages are circulating privately.

The intuition: a fixed strip buys a power saving for primes

The error in counting primes up to x is controlled by how far right the zeta zeros can sit. If none has real part above θ, the error is at most about xθ. Classical results only keep zeros a shrinking sliver away from 1, which saves less than any power of x. A fixed strip at 11/12 gives a genuine power saving. The Riemann hypothesis would give x1/2. The key move, which Sarnak had long advocated, is to study zeta inside a large family of related functions built from metaplectic forms. Averages over a family are much easier to control than any single function.

Sources: Quanta, “AI Proved the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis. What Happens Now?”.

A sharper provable attack on ML-KEM, with no change to its security

  • Preprint: IACR ePrint 2026/2565

On 20 October Kaveh Bashiri of Germany’s Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) posted “Further Elaborations on the Provable Dual Attack”. It doesn’t mention AI, but it is the fortnight’s most substantive lattice cryptanalysis and bears on last week’s AI-versus-lattices debate. It gives a dual attack on LWE that recovers part of the secret, with a proven lower bound on its success probability. It removes the informal assumptions left in earlier provable versions by Pouly and Shen and by Bashiri and Wiemers. Against ML-KEM, under one heuristic about its structured matrix, the paper reports gains of up to 22–52 bits over the first provable dual attack. It reports up to 47–100 bits if the sublattice rank is bounded as in a recent paper by Qu, Tian, Wang and Xu.

The intuition: short dual vectors turn noise into a test

LWE hides a secret s in b = As + e mod q, where e is small noise. A dual attack finds many short vectors w for which wA is short too. Guess part of s and form the remainder. For the right guess, each inner product with w is a sum of small numbers, so the values cluster near 0. For wrong guesses they look uniform. The hard part is proving that no wrong guess scores well by luck. In 2023 Ducas and Pulles showed the usual independence assumptions could be contradictory. This paper controls the wrong candidates’ scores using the exact uniform distribution of certain cosets, so it doesn’t need those assumptions.

For deployed systems: the author states plainly that the results do not threaten ML-KEM or ML-DSA. The gains are measured against an earlier provable attack, which was far weaker than the heuristic attacks used to set the parameters. My take: this narrows the gap between what is proved and what is believed.

Sources: Bashiri, ePrint 2026/2565, Qu, Tian, Wang and Xu, ePrint 2026/2117.

Follow-up: the Yang–Mills committee sets its terms

  • Company claim: OpenAI
  • Expert review: Gross-led committee, examining
  • Machine-checked: Lean promised, not released

What changed. The committee holding OpenAI’s Yang–Mills proof has published a statement through the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics. Its members are Michael Douglas, David Gross (chair), Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov. They say they work for the academic community: “We have no responsibility to OpenAI.” They describe the proof, which they say builds on Balaban’s renormalization-group work, as apparently an important step toward a rigorous footing for quantum field theory. Confinement and the mass gap remain open. Their plan has three stages. First, they will check that the Lean definitions and problem statement match the standard ones and that the formal proof matches the informal one. Then they will release the Lean proof, and finally their assessment with the full 1,147 pages of model-written proofs.

The intuition: why the definitions come first

Lean guarantees that a proof proves its stated theorem, but nothing more. For a quantum field theory, the statement is roughly this: a certain probability measure on gauge fields exists and satisfies a list of axioms (symmetry, positivity, and so on), from which a genuine quantum theory can be rebuilt. If one of those definitions is subtly too weak, the theorem could be true and still not mean “Yang–Mills exists”. That’s why the committee checks definitions before anything else.

Sources: Committee statement (KITP, PDF).

Data mining finds 33 overlooked MXenes and makes 11 new ones

  • Peer-reviewed: Science

On 29 October a team from Linköping University, working with colleagues in Estonia and Finland, published “Data-driven discovery and rapid, direct synthesis of MXenes” in Science. The group is led by Johanna Rosén and the first author is Ali Saffar Shamshirgar. AI-assisted searches of materials databases and the literature turned up 33 MXenes that had been made before but overlooked, and these findings guided the synthesis of 11 new ones. The team also reports a synthesis route that takes seconds with no external heating and uses fewer hazardous chemicals and less water. Rare-earth metals built into the structure make the sheets magnetic.

The intuition: a big chemical space whose map is scattered across papers

MXenes are sheets of metal carbide or nitride only a few atoms thick. The classic recipe starts from a layered “MAX phase” crystal and chemically etches out the A layers, often with hydrofluoric acid, leaving stacked sheets. Choices of metal, carbon or nitrogen, and surface chemistry form a large combinatorial space, and what is known is scattered across thousands of papers, so results get overlooked. Systematic mining is cheap compared with lab work. Caveat: the university’s release doesn’t describe the AI method or how each new compound was characterized, and I couldn’t access the paper itself.

Sources: phys.org on the Linköping release. Paper DOI: 10.1126/science.aeh5957.
